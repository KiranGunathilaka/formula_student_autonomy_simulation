Lidar Statistics

Which lidar should the ADS-DV carry? Each candidate drove the same rule-legal lap in Gazebo, and every frame of its simulated point cloud was counted on every cone. The car drove on its own pose, so each lidar is also judged on how well the car knew where it was.

Gazebo 11 with GPU-rendered lidars (RTX 5060) · ADS-DV, 3.0 m/s target speed · lidar_test_track: 392 m driven, 217 cones, four hairpins · 9 lidars · one lap each

Results

  • The ATX knows the track furthest ahead. For 95% of lap 1 it had at least 38.5 m of track ahead fully in its map, and a median of 58.1 m. The best spinning lidar, the Ouster OS2-128, had 16.9 m and 20.1 m; the VLP-16 we own had 5.0 m and 6.6 m.
  • The ATX had every cone of the lap in its map 47 m before the finish (346 m into the 392 m lap). The earliest spinning lidar, the Ouster OS2-128, got there 35 m before the finish; the VLP-16 9 m before.
  • It sees each cone from further away. Half the cones first met the rule more than 44.0 m from the ATX (90th percentile 52.0 m). The Ouster OS2-128: 17.3 m; the VLP-16: 5.4 m.
  • The detection rule does not change the order at the top. Under all three rules the ATX has the longest median known-ahead distance and first-detection range.
  • Why: beam spacing. The ATX spaces its 256 channels about 0.08° apart, 25 times closer than the VLP-16 and about 12 times closer than a 32-channel lidar, so a cone far ahead still crosses several of its beams. Its 120° view costs little: the cones beside and behind the car are already in the map.
  • Localisation: the car drove on its own pose, and with every lidar it knew it to within centimetres. Over 9 laps per lidar, the 95th percentile of the position error was 0.04 m with the Ouster OS2-128 and 0.16 m with the Velodyne VLP-16 (owned) (medians over the laps); wheel speeds and the IMU alone drifted up to 1.35 m. Ranked, with lidars sharing a place where their laps overlap: 1: Ouster OS2-128; 2–9: Ouster OS1-64, Hesai ATX, Hesai XT32, Ouster OS1-32, RoboSense Helios 5515, RoboSense Helios 1615, Ouster OS1-128, Velodyne VLP-16 (owned). A lidar ranks below a group only if even its best lap was worse than every lap in that group. Localisation has the distributions and the lap-to-lap spread.
  • Map building: the Hesai ATX's map held the most track ahead. Median over the lap of how far ahead the SLAM map had every cone within 0.25 m of where it is: Hesai ATX 173 m, Ouster OS2-128 84 m, Ouster OS1-128 39 m, Ouster OS1-64 24 m, Hesai XT32 20 m, RoboSense Helios 1615 19 m, Ouster OS1-32 19 m, RoboSense Helios 5515 17 m, Velodyne VLP-16 13 m (medians over 4 laps each). The top lidar is the same at 0.1 m and 0.5 m. The finished map, which later laps drive on, was most accurate with the Ouster OS2-128 (0.04 m, 95th percentile) and least with the Velodyne VLP-16 (owned) (0.17 m). With a stricter detector (detector needs 10 points): Hesai ATX 45 m, Ouster OS2-128 17 m, Ouster OS1-128 13 m, Ouster OS1-64 9 m, Hesai XT32 7 m, RoboSense Helios 1615 6 m, Ouster OS1-32 6 m, RoboSense Helios 5515 6 m, Velodyne VLP-16 5 m. Its position error, 95th percentile, ran from 0.10 m (Ouster OS2-128) to 0.70 m (Ouster OS1-32), medians over the laps; the lidars that mapped only a few metres ahead drifted, up to 1.6 m in a lap (table 14). Map building has the score per lidar.

The test lap

The lap is built to the FS-AI 2026 trackdrive rules: straights no longer than 80 m, constant turns up to 50 m across, hairpins at least 9 m outside diameter, and a track at least 3 m wide (FS-AI 2026 rules, D8.1, p. 33). Cones are at most 5 m apart in the driving direction and closer in corners (cones specification, p. 2). It has a 60 m straight into a hairpin, two pairs of hairpins back to back, a 30 m constant turn, a chicane and a decreasing-radius turn; 392 m in all.

The hairpins are 13 m across, not the 9 m minimum. The ADS-DV can steer at most ±21°, so the tightest circle its outer front tyre can turn is 9.9 m across: it cannot drive a 9 m hairpin at all. It did drive 11 m hairpins in the simulator, but touched cones on the way round, and a lap that touches cones is not a fair test. The script that builds the lap is falcon_bringup/tracks/make_lidar_test_track.py.

What is measured

StatisticWhat it isWhy it matters
Detection ruleA cone counts as detected in a frame when the lidar puts at least N points on it, from at least K different channels. Three rules: 3 points; 10 points on 2 channels (used for the headline numbers); 20 points on 3 channels. 3 points only says "something is there". Two or more channels give the cluster a height, so it can be told from ground noise; a cone-shape fit wants more. Showing all three stops the result depending on one choice.
Known aheadAt every half metre of lap 1: how far along the driven path every cone is already in the map. The first cone ahead that has not met the rule in any frame so far ends it. Nothing is capped. Reported as the full distribution: lowest, 5th, 25th, median, 75th, 95th percentile, highest. It covers the lap up to the point where every cone of the lap is in the map, which is reported on its own ("map complete"): near the end of lap 1 the only cones left ahead are the ones seen at the start, so from there on the whole lap is known with any lidar. On lap 1, and in autocross, the car cannot plan past the first cone it has not seen. That point limits how fast it may drive into what lies beyond. The 5th percentile is the distance it had for 95% of the lap; the lowest value is the single worst moment.
First detectionThe distance from the lidar at which each cone first met the rule. 10th percentile, median, 90th percentile and furthest, over every cone. How far ahead a cone becomes a landmark.
Points on a coneMean points on a cone in view, by range band.The raw signal behind every other number.

How the numbers were made

  1. Drive. The car is the ADS-DV model (1.53 m wheelbase, ±21° steering) in the EUFS Gazebo simulator. The stack's own centreline planner and pure pursuit drive one lap at up to 3.0 m/s, on the car's own pose estimate (see Localisation). They steer on the cones the simulator reports within 20 m and 180°, not on the lidar under test, so every lidar gets nearly the same drive: the lines differ only by the localisation error, centimetres.
  2. Simulate the lidar. Gazebo renders every beam of the lidar's profile on the GPU, ten times a second, from its mount on the car, and publishes the point cloud a real lidar would. Every lidar gets all ten frames: the nodes share the clouds through 32 MB shared-memory segments. With the default transport a 3 MB ATX frame went as UDP fragments, and most ATX frames were lost. Near cones hide far ones, the car's body hides beams, and each range gets 2 cm of Gaussian noise. Each lidar's range is the figure its datasheet gives for a 10% reflective target (table below).
  3. Count. The cone_coverage node takes each cloud, places it on the map using the simulator's true car pose (not the car's estimate, so only the lidar is judged here), and counts the points within 0.25 m of each cone's axis and above the ground, and the channels they came from. It keeps every frame and writes them to frames.npz when the lap ends.
  4. Statistics. lidar_stats --runs applies each detection rule to those frames and computes the statistics above. scripts/lidar_stats_gazebo.sh runs all of it, one lidar after another, and records each run; the videos below are those recordings.

Mounts: the ATX on the front sensor plate, 0.57 m up (the 2026 CarMaker model's lidar height); every spinning lidar on a short post above it, 0.70 m up, level.

Localisation

In these runs nothing in the stack read the simulator's pose. The car found its own pose from what the ADS-DV and the IMU we plan to fit give it, and drove on it; the lidar under test fed the cone SLAM. The errors below are the car's estimate against the simulator's true pose, which only the evaluation read.

What the car is given

SensorSimulated asSource
ADS-DV wheel speedsAll four wheels every 10 ms, as unsigned whole rpm (1 rpm = 0.026 m/s on the 0.505 m wheel); each wheel's rolling speed at its own Ackermann angle. The tyre's true rolling radius is 1% larger than the one the odometry assumes: an assumption, as the EUFS wheel (505 mm) and the CAD tyre (515 mm) are 2% apart. VCU2AI_Speeds, Software Interface Specification v4.0, p. 9; read as uint16 rpm in fs-ai_api.c

The ADS-DV's own PCAN-GPS IMU and GPS, which the FS-AI API also reads, are not used, and neither is RTK. The recorded laps all used the same noise seed; the repeat laps (table 10) used others. The sensors come out on the topics the real drivers use (/ros_can/wheel_speeds as the EUFS/Bristol ros_can bridge, /imu/data as the Xsens driver), so the same localisation runs on the car.

How it works

  1. Odometry EKF (falcon_localization/odometry.py), 200 Hz. The gyro and the accelerometer predict heading and the body velocities. Each CAN frame then corrects them with all four wheels (each wheel's speed is the body velocity at that wheel along its heading). It also uses a soft "the rear axle does not slide" constraint. While all four wheels read 0 rpm at the start, the gyro's bias is learnt. On its own this is the "odometry alone" line below.
  2. Cone detector (lidar_cone_detector_3d.py). Points between 4 cm and 70 cm above the ground are clustered on a 15 cm grid, and clusters the size of a cone become detections without colour. Range: the lidar's full datasheet range. Points on the car itself are dropped by a box about 0.1 m bigger than the body, wide enough for the front tyres at full lock; cut exactly at the tail, range noise left points of the tail outside it, and the detector reported a cone just behind the car in every scan.
  3. Cone EKF-SLAM (falcon_localization/slam.py), every scan:
    • predicts with the odometry since the last scan;
    • pairs the detections with the mapped cones by nearest Mahalanobis distance, as one assignment, inside a χ² gate and no more than 1 m from where the mapped cone should be. The χ² gate alone is not enough: after a lap the car's uncertainty relative to the cones mapped at the start reaches metres, and with a detector that finds cones only within about 6 m, a cone the map did not hold yet paired with its neighbour one cone spacing on, and the car jumped 3–5 m at the loop closure;
    • corrects the pose and every mapped cone in one update.
    A new cone joins the map once it has been seen in 3 scans, from car positions at least 1 m apart, with its sightings staying together. Returns that move with the car never join.
  4. Evaluation (falcon_evaluation/localization.py). Every 0.05 s it records the estimate, the true pose, and the odometry alone (lined up with the truth at the start). Errors are taken every 0.5 m driven, so time standing still does not count. Like the rest of this page they are shown as full distributions, uncapped.

Map building

On lap 1 the planner drives on the SLAM's cone map, so the score for map building is how much of the track ahead that map gets right. Map ahead: every 0.5 m of the lap, the distance along the track to the first cone ahead that the map does not hold within 0.25 m of where it really is. Both positions are taken relative to the car: the map cone from the car's own pose, the true cone from the true pose, because that is where the planner puts it. A false map cone on the track ends it too. It is chart 1's "known ahead", which asks only whether the lidar's rays reached a cone, after the detector, the SLAM and the car's own pose have had their say. The lidars below are ranked by its median over the lap.

Why 0.25 m: the track can be 3 m wide (FS-AI 2026 rules, D8.1, p. 33) and the ADS-DV is 1.43 m wide (ADS-DV Specifications & Dimensions, §5). That leaves 0.79 m each side, and 0.25 m is a third of it, about one cone base (228 mm, cones specification, p. 2). The table also gives the score at 0.1 m and 0.5 m, so the ranking can be checked against the choice.

Limits

  • Simulator cones are slimmer than real ones. The EUFS cone models are 160 mm across at the base against the rulebook's 228 mm (cones specification, Table 2, p. 2), so every lidar puts fewer points on them than it would on a real cone.
  • Clean returns. Every beam that meets a cone returns a point with 2 cm of range noise: no reflectivity loss, rain, sun or dust. Real lidars lose some returns at range, more so the ones with fewer, wider beams.
  • Flat ground, level car. No pitch from braking or bumps.
  • One lap, one speed, one track. Each lidar drove the lap once at up to 3.0 m/s. A faster car gets fewer frames per metre; the distances would shrink a little for every lidar.
  • Geometry only. Cone colour, intensity and a detector's own false positives are not modelled; the rules stand in for a detector.
  • Localisation: tidy sensors. The car stays level, so the accelerometer never feels gravity from pitch or roll. Wheels slip only as the simulator's tyre model allows. CAN, IMU and lidar share one clock with no delay. Each Gazebo scan is taken at one instant, where a real spinning lidar sweeps for a tenth of a second while the car moves. The 1% tyre error is an assumption, and the IMU's noise and bias are its datasheet figures. Real numbers will be worse for every lidar. What these runs give is the lidars side by side under the same conditions, with the lap-to-lap spread showing which differences are real.
